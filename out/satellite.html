Most up-to-date satellite images: check the date

People searching for up-to-date satellite images, updated satellite images or the most recent satellite images usually want one thing: to know the photo shows the place as it is now. No free map offers a live satellite view, so the honest answer is to show the date. This map does that for every view: the imagery panel under the map gives the capture date, resolution and source of the exact photo you are looking at.

  • Big cities and landmarks: usually photographed within the last one to two years, as the table below shows.
  • Towns and suburbs: typically one to three years old.
  • Rural and remote areas: can be several years old, and zoomed out you see a global mosaic with no single date.
  • Need the latest satellite imagery? Compare with Google Maps satellite view using the link under the map. Different providers update different places, so one of them is often newer.

Because the date is always on screen, these are the most up-to-date satellite maps you can check for free without guessing: if the photo is too old for your purpose, you know straight away.

How old is the satellite image?

Satellite images of major cities on this map are usually less than two years old: the Eiffel Tower, the Statue of Liberty and the Pyramids of Giza were all photographed in 2025. Rural areas can be several years older. The tool shows the exact satellite image date for any point you look at.

Most satellite maps never tell you this. The satellite imagery here comes from Esri’s World Imagery mosaic, and Esri publishes metadata for every photo in it: the capture date, the resolution, the positional accuracy, and the provider. The tool reads that metadata for the point at the centre of your screen, at your current zoom, because a single view can mix several photos taken on different days.

What you can and cannot see

Resolution is the size of the patch of ground one pixel covers. In most towns the imagery here is 30 to 50 cm per pixel, which in practice means:

  • Clearly visible: buildings and roof shapes, driveways, gardens, swimming pools, cars (about ten pixels long), trees, paths, and field boundaries.
  • Just about visible: road markings, garden furniture, people as a dark dot or shadow.
  • Not visible: faces, number plates, house numbers, or anything under a roof or tree canopy.

Everything is seen from straight above, so walls and shopfronts do not appear. For the face of a building, use Street View, which shows the street-level photos instead.

Satellite or aerial photo?

“Satellite view” is the everyday name, but the sharpest imagery on any map is often taken from aircraft. Commercial satellites such as those operated by Vantor (formerly Maxar) photograph most of the world at around 30 to 50 cm per pixel. Planes fly lower and can capture 15 cm or finer, so many cities and national mapping programmes use aerial surveys instead. Esri’s mosaic mixes both: in New York, for example, its metadata lists a 15 cm aerial survey from 2024 for the very closest zoom level, above 50 cm satellite imagery for everything else. The imagery panel names the source for whatever you are looking at.

Why another map shows a different picture

Google Maps, Apple Maps, Bing Maps, and Esri each license imagery from different providers and update different places at different times. The same house can appear in summer on one map and in winter on another, with or without a new extension, or with a neighbouring building that has since been knocked down. Shadows point in different directions, and tall buildings lean differently, because each photo was taken from a slightly different angle. When the date matters, the “Compare in Google Maps” link under the map opens the same point in Google satellite view so you can check both.

When satellite view beats a regular map

  • Checking what a property and its surroundings look like before you visit, rent, or buy.
  • Confirming a building actually exists at an address you have been given.
  • Finding the right entrance, gate, or car park on a large site for a delivery or a meeting.
  • Planning a walk or hike where the map lacks detail but the photo shows tracks and clearings.
  • Seeing context a map leaves out: is the place in a desert, next to water, surrounded by forest?

For the opposite job, a clean labelled map for finding your way, the interactive map is a better fit, and Driving Directions plans the route there.

Where the imagery and searches come from

The photos and their metadata come from Esri’s World Imagery service, which combines imagery from Vantor, Earthstar Geographics, government mapping agencies, and other contributors. The place-label and road overlays are Esri reference layers built from HERE, Garmin, and OpenStreetMap data. Address searches use OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim geocoder; coordinates are read in your browser and never sent anywhere. The map requests imagery tiles and metadata for the area you are viewing from Esri’s servers, as every web map does. More detail is in our Privacy Policy.

Frequently asked questions

Is this Google Maps satellite view?

It works the same way, but the photos come from Esri's World Imagery rather than Google, and this map tells you the date each photo was taken, which Google Maps does not. To see Google's own picture of the same spot, press "Open this view in Google Maps satellite view" under the map. Both are free.

How do I see a satellite view of my location?

Press "My location" above the map and allow location access. The map centres on where you are, at street level, with the capture date of the photo shown under it. Your position stays in your browser and is not sent to us.

Is there a free alternative to Google Earth satellite view?

Yes. This page shows free satellite and aerial imagery of the whole world in your browser, with no download and no account, and opens any spot in Google Maps or Google Street View with one click. Google Earth adds 3D buildings and historical imagery; this map adds the capture date and source of every photo.

Is this a live satellite view?

No, and no free service is. The detailed imagery on this page, on Google Maps, and on Bing Maps is a mosaic of photos taken months or years ago. The panel under the map tells you exactly when the photo you are looking at was taken. Satellites that do image the whole Earth continuously, such as weather satellites, see details of around half a kilometre at best, enough for clouds but not for buildings.

How do I see my house from a satellite?

Type your address into the search box and press Search, or press "My location" to centre the map on where you are. The map zooms to street level. Turn on Place labels or Roads if you need help getting your bearings, and click the map to drop a pin on the exact building.

Why does Google Maps show a different picture of my house?

Google, Apple, Bing, and Esri buy imagery from different providers and update different areas at different times, so the same address can show a different season, a different year, or even a building that has since been demolished. Use "Compare in Google Maps" under the map to open the same spot in Google's satellite view.

How detailed is the imagery?

In most towns and cities it is 30 to 50 cm per pixel, so a car covers roughly ten pixels: you can see cars, trees, swimming pools, and roof shapes, but not number plates or faces. Some cities have aerial photography down to about 15 cm. Remote areas can be much coarser, and zoomed out you see a 15 m global mosaic.

Can I ask for my house to be blurred?

Satellite and aerial imagery providers generally do not blur individual homes on request, because the photos are taken from directly above and do not show anything a passer-by could not see. Street-level photos are different: Google Street View and Apple Look Around both accept blur requests for houses, faces, and number plates.
